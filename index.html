Worldview

four questions i keep asking · tap to unfold

I started with a stupidly simple question: why does everyone agree to call this an apple? Not how the word got here — but why we all settled on the same one. Language takes something as personal as a feeling and turns it into a fixed symbol. Something always gets crushed in that translation.

from notebook, 2024

That's the plot of every dystopia I love. In 1984, shrink the language and you shrink what people can think. In Animal Farm, the horror isn't that things are bad — it's that nobody dares say they're bad. A "perfect" world where the definition froze is already dead. We just haven't noticed yet.

reading notes on four dystopias

We all agree the sun rises in the east. That's the shared part. But I think it looks like tomato jam and you think it looks like blueberry jam, and neither of us can prove the other wrong — even if we both say "red." Consensus doesn't mean we see the same thing. That's not a bug.

notebook, "red sun blue sun"

Language is powerful enough to shape reality. So why can't it be a source of energy? In the world I'm building, every conversation makes a kind of energy — yǔnéng. Strong definitions become tools, weak ones become fuel for the city. A boss snaps at someone lower and the room carries it. That's not magic. It's just admitting language has weight.

worldbuilding note, "linguistic energy"

Kulatish — a language I built

← back to section
since 202312 chapters25‑sign script~400 roots
Why"When I was little, English clauses felt backwards. You don't find out who did it till the very end of the sentence. I didn't think English was wrong — I thought maybe English speakers just… wait. Like their brains hold the door open. So I made a language where the rules bend around how you actually look at something, not the other way round."
What
SOV word order, nominative–accusative alignment. Adjective order follows the sequence a human actually notices features in — size, shape, age, color. A 25‑sign ancestor script sits underneath as the "ancient layer," and derivational affixes let you weld root concepts into new words.
Outcome
A working language with a base‑12 numeral system, a logographic script, and a full grammar manual (PDF, 45 pp.). Sample texts: a folk tale, a dialogue, a philosophical fragment.
Reflection & Next Steps
Expanding the lexicon and writing a short story entirely in Kulatish with interlinear gloss. Next: record a spoken sample and test mutual intelligibility with another conlanger.

Ch.1 · Phonology & Orthography

23 consonants, 7 vowels, (C)V(C) syllables, stress rules, Latin‑based romanization with IPA guide.

Ch.2 · Noun Declension (5 cases)

Nominative, accusative, genitive, dative, locative. Regular suffixes + short irregular list.

Ch.3 · Verb Conjugation

Tense × aspect × mood: past/present/future, perfective/imperfective, indicative/imperative/subjunctive/conditional.

Ch.4 · Adjective Ordering

Perceptual hierarchy: size → shape → age → color → origin → material → purpose. Swap it and the phrase goes ungrammatical.

Ch.5 · Numeral System (base 12)

Roots 0–11, compounding, ordinals, distributives. Base 12 chosen for divisibility by 2,3,4,6.

Ch.6 · Clause Embedding

Relative, complement, adverbial clauses. Subordinating particles and word‑order shifts.

Ch.7 · Discourse Particles

Topic marker, focus particle, evidentials (direct vs hearsay), politeness registers.

Ch.8 · Derivational Morphology

Verb→noun, noun→adjective, causative/benefactive/reflexive affixes with before‑after examples.

Ch.9 · 25‑Sign Ancestor Script

Logographic layer: each sign = one root concept. Decipherment notes + 3 sample inscriptions.

Ch.10 · Lexicon (~400 roots)

Semantic fields: nature, body, action, emotion, time, space, social relations. Each with gloss and example.

Ch.11 · Register & Pragmatics

Formal/informal, honorifics, hesitation markers, fillers. Indirectness strategies.

Ch.12 · Sample Texts

Folk tale, dialogue, philosophical fragment. Interlinear gloss + idiomatic English translation.

🜃 The Ancient Layer — branching outward, converging inward

Underneath modern Kulatish sits an older system I call Quratish-thol, the "root tongue." Its 25 signs don't name words — they name directions a concept can move. Every modern word is built from one of those directions plus a modifier, which is why the lexicon feels like a map with 25 compass points.

The logic is outward‑branch, inward‑converge: from a single root the meaning sprouts into neighbouring domains — fire reaches toward heat, toward light, toward anger, toward hearth. Follow any branch far enough and it meets a branch from another root, and at that crossing the language forces a choice — one word survives, the other becomes an obsolete glyph. That's how the ancient script shrank from dozens of signs to the 25 that remain.

I think of it as the opposite of how definitions usually work. A dictionary closes a word in. Quratish-thol opens it out, then waits for two open ends to collide. The place they collide is where a new word is born — and where an old one quietly dies.

① Aphasia Observation — speech therapy shadowing

← back to section
Jan 2026 · winter breakBeijing · PKU Medical Rehab Hospital
Why"In the world I'm writing, a god silences everyone because they defined everything into stillness. Then I walked into a real hospital and saw people whose words had broken — and they weren't silent at all. They tapped rhythms, drew shapes, held eye contact a beat too long. Language wasn't gone. It had just rerouted. That changed how I think about 'broken.'"
What
Sat in on speech therapy sessions, logging how people rebuild communication when words fail — gesture, rhythm, drawings in the air. No equipment. Just watching what the body does when the mouth can't.
Outcome
Notebook with 12 sessions documented. Key pattern: patients consistently used spatial mapping (pointing, tracing shapes) before attempting vocalisation.
Reflection & Next
Returning next winter for a prosody‑focused second round (hospital approved). Also adapting one technique into a workshop for my drama club.

Notes & environment only. No patient name, diagnosis or record pictured. Observing done with department permission and patients' awareness.

② Dialect Fieldwork — Yuxian, Shanxi

← back to section
Aug 2026 · summer breakYushe County, Shanxi
Why"A dying dialect isn't just words disappearing. It's a whole way of noticing the world slowing down. I didn't go there to 'save' it. I went to watch what happens when a town's definitions start freezing — and to catch the ones still melting."
What
Recorded local speakers and chased the in‑between variety — a dialect halfway to Mandarin. Three patterns: tone merging, one word pronounced two ways, and quiet resolutions made in a blink.
Outcome
Audio of 8 speakers, a tone‑log spreadsheet, and a short paper on tone mergers. Shared with the local cultural archive.
Reflection & Next
Going back summer 2027 with a spectrogram setup. The younger generation barely uses the dialect — the window is closing.

③ Drug Package Insert Redesign — for my grandmother

← back to section
May–Jun 2025submitted to CDE, NMPA
Why"My grandmother hides from side effects. She reads 'may cause dizziness' and decides the pill is poison. But the real poison is the font size and the lawyer‑speak. She skipped her heart meds for three days because the warning scared her more than the disease. So I rewrote it — pictures instead of paragraphs, colours instead of footnotes. One insert shouldn't decide whether someone lives or skips."
What
Redesigned the drug package insert: pictograms instead of dense paragraphs, colour‑coded dosing, plain‑language warnings. Surveyed 20+ elderly patients, then submitted the proposal to the Center for Drug Evaluation, NMPA (CDE).
Outcome
Proposal accepted for review by CDE. 85% of participants preferred the new design. A small pilot print run is being discussed with a local pharmacy.
Reflection & Next
Building a general‑purpose template for any chronic‑disease medication. Next: user‑test with n=100 across three hospitals, then revise the submission.

Personified

giving everything a personality · how i remember the abstract

Anthropomorphic Thinking — nothing is too small to have a voice

← back to section
ongoinga habit, not a project
Why"I don't memorise well. But if a subway line has a personality, I remember its route. If a chemical element has a social role, I remember its properties. It's not a trick — it's how I connect to things that otherwise feel abstract. Everything has a voice if you listen the right way."
What
I understand things by imagining them as people. Give a subway line a temper and I'll know which stops it rushes; give an element a social life and I'll know how it bonds. Two favourites below — I've done dozens more.
Outcome
A running sketchbook of "character sheets" for the things around me. They've become my default study tool: transit maps, the periodic table, grammar cases, even punctuation marks.
Reflection & Next
Turning the best ones into a small illustrated zine, and testing whether the method helps other people — especially visual learners — hold onto dry facts.

Shanghai Metro Line 1

"The old auntie who's been running this route since 1993. She's tired but she won't admit it. Her doors close slower than the others, like she's waiting for you — and then she sighs and leaves anyway."

Knowing her temper is why I can draw the whole line from memory: Xinzhuang to Fujin Road, every sulky pause in between.

The Noble Gases

"They don't react because they're complete. Argon is the quiet kid in the back who already knows everything and never raises a hand. Helium is the one who talks too fast and floats away before you can answer."

A whole family dinner: neon shows off, krypton broods, xenon plays the generous host. Their refusal to bond stops being a table fact and starts being a personality trait.
