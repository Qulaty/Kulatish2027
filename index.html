Kulatish · the 12 chapters first

Kulatish — a language I built

↑ top of section
since 202412 chapters · the core25-sign ancient scriptworking draft
Why

When I was little, English clauses felt backwards — you don't find out who did it till the very end of the sentence. I didn't think English was wrong; I thought maybe English speakers just wait, their brains holding the door open. So I made a language where the rules bend around how you actually look at something, not the other way round.

What I did

Twelve chapters of grammar — word order, case, aspect, modality, adjective ordering, negation, questions, measure, discourse particles, clauses — plus a separate hand-drawn layer of 25 signs on a 4×4 board.

What I take away

The tidy grammar pins meaning down; the 25 signs leave the reader room to fill it in. Keeping both taught me that a shared rule and an open gap can sit inside the same system.

Next

Build an input method so I can type whole sentences in the 25 signs, then feed those sentences to a model and see whether it picks up the logic.

Each heading is a rule I had to decide on. Where the grammar could have gone another way, I've marked the choice and the reason — no rule here is inherited from a natural language.

02 · The nounFive cases for who holds, who receives, who's affected, where, and with what.
03 · Tense & aspectSplit apart. Time says when; aspect says how the action fills its space.
04 · ModalityWhat must, may or almost did — the grammar of being unsure.
05 · AdjectivesAn ordered queue: size, then colour, then origin. No exceptions by mood.
06 · Verbs & valenceHow many partners an action wants, and what changes when you add one.
07 · NegationThe rule I keep coming back to: negation isn't a word but a position, and it flips whatever scope it sits inside. It's the one place the grammar admits it can't settle a sentence on its own.
08 · QuestionsMarked by intent, not word order. A demand for an answer reads differently.
09 · Number & measureBase-12 counting, plus classifiers only where the thing's shape matters.
10 · Discourse particlesThe tiny bits that say "I'm serious," "between us," or "you already know."
11 · ClausesEmbedding, switching viewpoint, and how a sentence survives its own detour.
12 · Writing it downThe 25 signs aren't the alphabet — they're an option panel. This chapter bridges the two.

Dialect fieldwork · Yuxian, Shanxi

↑ top of section
Aug 2026Yuxian, ShanxiSummer break
Why

Yuxian County in Shanxi is known for holding on to Middle Chinese features, and I was curious whether that "holding on" was something speakers chose or something that just happened to them.

What I did

Summer 2026, three generations of one family and their neighbours. I recorded comparisons, noted which tones were drifting, and asked what the young people — the ones who'd come back home after working away — no longer said at the dinner table.

What I take away

The drift isn't random: parents smooth their talk toward children, kids bring the standard home, and the blurry one-sound-many-meanings bits survive because context sorts them out. I came looking for a language "holding on"; I found people deciding what to keep.

Next

Transcribe the recordings and see whether the tone shifts line up with who talks to whom most.

Reading the drift
Change tracks social use, not decay: parents smooth their talk toward children, kids bring the standard home, and a few rounds later the local forms converge toward Mandarin. The opaque, one-sound-many-meanings bits stay because context resolves them in a blink — efficiency, not loss.
A field note
Faces and names stay out of frame. Watching the clips back, I kept noticing that understanding landed in a gesture or a laugh before it landed in a word — which is exactly the gap this project is about.
What the drift says
It isn't random. Change tracks social trust and efficiency: people collectively decide what's worth keeping.
A compromise phonology in progress. Parents Mandarinate their talk to children; kids bring the standard home; a few rounds later the drift converges.
The opaque bits stay. One-sound-many-meanings quirks are resolved by context in a blink — holding them back is what makes talk efficient.
“My first guess was that the dialect was trapping people's thinking. What I saw instead was a community, together and on purpose, deciding which language to use next.”field note · Yuxian, Aug 2026

What I want to keep doing: turn up with a hypothesis, then let the recordings — and the people — rewrite it. The space between intention and reception is where the habit shows up.

Aphasia Observation · speech therapy shadowing

↑ top of section
Jan 2026BeijingWinter break
Why

In the world I'm writing, a god silences everyone because they defined everything into stillness. Then I walked into a real hospital and saw people whose words had broken — and they weren't silent at all. They tapped rhythms, drew shapes, held eye contact a beat too long. Language wasn't gone; it had just rerouted. That changed how I think about "broken."

What I did

Shadowed a speech-language therapist for two weeks: picture-naming and word-card tasks with people who had aphasia, and the swallowing work that sits inside the same job. No equipment — just watching what the body does when the mouth can't.

What I take away

At first I was afraid to talk to the patients. I learned to hold the eye contact, smile, and wait longer than feels natural. A pause isn't empty — it's where the next word is being assembled.

Next

Learn the standard assessment scales properly, so "waiting" can turn into something more useful than patience. Also adapting one technique into a workshop for my drama club.

On waiting
“He could only say two words. I learned to wait longer than feels natural.” They were Eat. and Home. — after a stroke, he understood everything else and would nod back. That silence taught me more than any textbook.

Notes & environment only. No patient name, diagnosis or record pictured. Observing done with department permission and patients' awareness. At my desk I only care whether a grammar is elegant; in that ward I saw that breaking language breaks identity — and the meaning was still there, unspoken.

Drug Package Insert · the elder-friendly design

↑ top of section
May–Jun 2025Shanghai20+ participants interviewed
Why

I usually live with my grandmother. My mother works in Beijing and comes home at weekends, so reminding her, checking the doses and managing changes in her medicine is something I do. Once, after a new prescription, she wanted to read the leaflet first. It listed frightening side effects in words she could not follow, and she was too scared to take the pills.

What I did

My interest in the chemical formulas printed on drug labels made me look closer at the language around them. I compared how other countries keep a professional monograph separate from a plain-language patient leaflet, tested a simplified draft by reading it aloud with my grandmother and her friends, then wrote five concrete suggestions.

What I take away

What older readers need is a safe way to use the medicine — not the pharmacology. The chemistry on the box is the easy part; the words around it decide whether someone actually takes the pill.

Next

On 19 June 2025 I emailed the five points to the Center for Drug Evaluation (CDE), NMPA during the public consultation. Next I would take a revised draft back to my grandmother's friends and see whether it reads the way they do.

Everything pictured here is redacted: no patient name, diagnosis, address or case number appears. The gap between what the leaflet says and what a patient hears is exactly the gap this project is trying to close.

Punctuation People

39 marks and counting

I gave punctuation marks personalities, and the story stopped belonging to me. A period's job is to end things, so Parid is the one who always closes the door; a tilde drifts, so Tiao can't stay still. Once the functions had tempers, the characters started answering each other instead of waiting for me to write them.

What I take away

The cast is now bigger than the page I drew it on. Parentheses, slashes and the rest of the ellipsis family are still being finished.

Metro Society

taking the metro is how I learned to notice people

20 lines, around 500 stations

I turned 20 Shanghai lines into characters — colour, opening year, and the neighbourhoods each one passes through became their temper, job and relations. I rode every line and stopped at roughly 500 stations. A line's colour is the easy part to personify; harder is deciding what it does with the city it carries.

Why this started
Line 1 is steady, like a mother; Line 5 is the daughter who's always catching up. Drawing them slowed me down at stations — I started listening to how strangers divide a carriage. The group portrait is tall on purpose: every line gets its own footing, nothing cropped out.

I've loved the metro since I was little. The drawings are rough, and that's the point: each line's temperament comes from something small I saw on a platform, not from any skill with a brush.

Chemistry

a study sheet where the atoms have faces

Covalent Bonds, drawn like a comic

This is one page from my stack of subject notebooks — the chemistry one. I'd got tired of memorising the rules, so I turned covalent bonding into four small moves. The molecules got personalities along the way.

To draw a covalent bond, all you need are four steps. One: count the total valence electrons. Two: decide which atom sits in the middle. Three: draw the bonds. Four: check the octet rule.

I added examples of single, double and triple bonds — H₂, O₂ and N₂ — and gave the shared electrons their own little circles, because a bond looks much friendlier when you can see the pair.

Then the characters snuck in. The little girl in the crown is HNO₃: she explains how nitric acid holds together. The cool boy in the trench coat is HCl, demoing the bonding rule. While I was at it, they show why atoms in periods 1 and 2 must satisfy the octet — and why period 3 and beyond get to bend it.

And whatever you do, don't miss the red slogan in the bottom-left corner: "stable is goal." Because in the end, that's what every molecule is chasing. I hope you like the sheet. Stay stable 😊

Stage & Fiction

Grade 11 · 2025–2026
Theatre

G11  ·  20+ members  ·  writer, director, actor  ·  English script

Meaning isn't encoded — it's something people do together. Putting personified grammar on a stage was the quickest way to test whether a mark's "temper" could hold up in a room. Grade 11, 2025–2026: I wrote, directed and acted as drama-club president; a staged reading of the grammar characters went to the district festival, and the club was named best school club of the year.

Why

I once made two characters say the exact same line but breathe it differently. The room split. That's pragmatics — meaning lives in the pause, not the word. You can't prove that in an essay; you can prove it at 9pm on a sticky floor.

What I did

Wrote and directed original pieces about language and power, and acted in them. Rehearsals are where I check whether my theories survive contact with a real, tired, laughing audience.

What I take away

The best pauses weren't mine. A missed cue or a laugh from the cast landed harder than anything I'd blocked. The abstraction stopped being mine alone.

Next

A longer run, with the audience's reactions written back into the script.

Two posters, five shots from the stage. No video of the run exists — just the photographs and what the audience told us afterwards. Every missed cue, every pause that landed differently from how I'd blocked it: that was the habit live in a room, no longer mine to control.

Reflections

three habits the work has taught me

Three habits I keep coming back to when a project stops going to plan.

01 · assumptions I dropped

The dialect trip is the clearest one.

My first guess was that the dialect was cramping how people think. The recordings said otherwise. I followed the evidence, corrected the hypothesis, and saw the point: language is there to be understood. What a community cares about is being able to talk to one another, so a local dialect naturally leans toward the common tongue.

02 · the project that changed me most

Being understood matters more than being correct.

The ward is where language stopped being a system I could take apart for fun. I still want to describe how meaning works — how a sign, a pause or a tone lands. But I now care just as much about who that meaning is working for, and whether the person on the other end can reach it.

03 · what I'm still figuring out

Colinta — difference is the point, a shared rule is the deal.

Two threads stay live. One is the 25-sign board for Kulatish, which I'd like to turn into a working input method. The other is a plain-language drug leaflet I can actually send in. Same question underneath both: communication needs a common rule, or no one meets; it needs difference too, or there's nothing worth exchanging. Entropy isn't noise — it's the price of something being said at all.

About Me

grade 12, IB · a lover of linguistics, building with it

IOLC — International Linguistics Olympiad in China.

I first met IOLC in grade 10 and was hooked at once — the problems read like codes to crack. I taught myself the material from scratch, sat the Junior Level, and came away with Merit in both the National Individual and National Team rankings. Five hours of written paper, not tired at all; just thoroughly enjoying it.

In grade 11 I moved up to Senior Level and took Merit in the National Team ranking.

IOLC is where my interest in linguistics began, and one reason I went on to build a language of my own and to keep watching the real-world questions around how people use language. I've just entered the Senior Level for the 2027 round, sitting next March.

Between symbols, linguistic energy thrives. Differences and uncertainty enliven while raising communication's entropy. I am Karl from Shanghai, exploring linguistics, psychology, cognitive science, or philosophy, hoping to better understand people.
